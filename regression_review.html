Vols 1–3: regression, multiple regression, multilevel models

How this review was done

I read all six files — three lectures and three practicals, about 9,900 lines — and ran code where a claim could be checked rather than taken on trust. Every number quoted below was produced by running something, not read off a slide. Where I say a link or a path is broken, I checked it.

The headline: this is a strong, unusually honest three-lecture series with a real case study and a genuine argument running through it. Most of what follows is small corrections and one substantial recommendation about grouping factors. The Python problem in Volume 3 turns out to be solvable without changing packages, and that is in its own section.

What is good — and worth protecting

These are the things I would not change, because they are what makes the series work.

The recipe framing earns its keep. “Step 1 — Ingredients” spends an entire third of Volume 1 on EDA before a model is fitted, and the slide that says the process of exploring your data should take much longer than the modelling at the end is the single most useful sentence in the series. Most regression teaching inverts this.

The case study has real stakes. Brighton and Hove Council made an actual admissions policy decision on the basis of Gorard’s work, and the lectures are explicitly an argument about whether the evidence supports it. Students are not modelling a toy dataset; they are auditing a live policy. The “So the council was right? Well, not quite…” beat in Volume 1 is the moment the series stops being a stats course.

“A regression model is nothing more than a description of a scatter plot.” This is the right first sentence about regression and it is followed properly — line of best fit, residuals, R², slope and intercept all built visually from the same plot before any algebra appears.

The degrees-of-freedom sequence is excellent. Building from 2 points to 3 to 4 to 5, showing the line pivoting, makes “degrees of freedom” a thing you can see rather than a number in an output. I have not seen it taught this way elsewhere and it works.

The deliberate failure in Volume 1. Fitting on 10 Brighton schools, getting a satisfying R² of 0.69, and then showing that three plausible policy changes flatten the relationship to nothing — and that the model goes statistically insignificant — is the best teaching in the series. It makes overfitting visceral instead of abstract.

The diagnostics scoreboard carried from Volume 1 into Volume 2, with the emoji going from 😭 to 😎 as each assumption gets addressed, gives the two lectures a spine. Students always know where they are.

The VIF intuition. “If two variables are highly correlated and both appear to affect the outcome, the model makes an arbitrary split. Small changes in the data could make the split go either way — thus inflating the variance.” That is the clearest one-paragraph explanation of multicollinearity I have read in a course.

“ALWAYS MAP YOUR RESIDUALS IF MODELLING SPATIAL DATA”, with the London effect visible on the map, is a lesson that will outlive the course.

The confounding section quantifies itself. Showing the disadvantage coefficient shrink from −0.21 to −0.11 (44.5%) while absence shrinks from −0.54 to −0.41 (24.6%), and concluding that disadvantage is almost twice as biased by the omission, is exactly the right level of rigour.

The Gabber/Hakken mediation example is genuinely funny and will be remembered. Keep it.

Volume 3’s shrinkage treatment — that groups with few observations get pulled toward the global mean, and that this is a feature which avoids overfitting to small groups — is well judged, and the paired visualisations do real work.

Things that are wrong and should be fixed

1. The p-value is defined correctly and then immediately inverted

Volume 1 gets it right first:

The \(P\)-value — probability of observing a t-value as extreme as the one calculated if there were no relationship between the independent and the dependent variable

and then, on the same slide:

A small \(P\)-value (typically \(p\) < 0.05) indicates <5% chance that \(X\) is not really explaining variation in \(Y\)

and on the next slide:

\(P\) <0.001 = <0.1% \(P\)-robability that 62.3 occurred by random chance

The second and third statements are the inverse-probability fallacy — they say \(P(\text{no relationship} \mid \text{data})\) when the p-value is \(P(\text{data} \mid \text{no relationship})\). This is the most common misconception about p-values, students will carry it into their dissertations, and it is being stated here immediately after the correct definition.

Suggested fix — keep the first definition and rephrase the others as:

A small p-value means data like ours would be surprising if there were really no relationship. It does not tell us the probability that there is no relationship — for that you would need to know how likely a relationship was before you looked.

2. The standard-error interval is a 68% interval presented as if it were the interval

SE of 0.15 for the estimate of −0.63 means the change could vary between −0.48 and −0.78

That is estimate ± 1 SE, which is roughly a 68% interval. The conventional 95% interval is ±1.96 SE, i.e. −0.34 to −0.92 — nearly twice as wide, and a much more honest picture of the uncertainty in a model with 8 degrees of freedom. Either widen it or say explicitly that this is a one-standard-error range.

3. Comparing predictor “importance” by t-value

Both Volume 1 and Volume 2 use t-values to rank predictors:

t-values indicate that the % Overall Absence variable (−50.49) has a stronger effect than the % Disadvantaged Students variable (−31.16)

A t-value is \(\hat\beta/\text{SE}\), so it confounds effect size with precision. With n ≈ 3,000 both are overwhelmingly significant and the ratio mostly reflects how well each coefficient is pinned down, not how much each matters.

There is a better move available, and the lecture has already set it up. Both variables are logged, so their coefficients are elasticities and are directly comparable: a 1% rise in absence is associated with a 0.41% fall in Attainment 8, against 0.11% for disadvantage. That is a cleaner comparison than the t-values, it needs no caveat, and it reinforces the elasticity idea introduced a few slides earlier.

4. Ofsted rating is the wrong grouping factor for Volume 3

This is the one substantive recommendation in this review.

Volume 3 uses OFSTEDRATING as the random effect throughout — the null model, the ICC, the random-intercept model and the random-slope model. There are two problems.

It has too few groups, and two are tiny. Counting from the data (the figures below are for the Attainment 8 subset; on the Progress 8 rows the lecture actually models, the two smallest groups are smaller still, at 16 and 22 schools):

Ofsted rating Schools
Good 2,235
Outstanding 468
Requires improvement 407
Serious Weaknesses 44
Special Measures 32

Five groups is at the very bottom of what will support a between-group variance estimate — the usual guidance is a minimum of five and a preference for considerably more — and two of them are small enough that their intercepts are almost entirely shrinkage. The lecture teaches shrinkage beautifully and then picks a grouping factor where shrinkage does nearly all the work.

And it is close to circular. An Ofsted rating is in part a judgement about attainment. Modelling attainment as clustered within Ofsted rating asks how much of the variation in attainment is explained by a grading of attainment. This is precisely the circularity the new GLM lecture (Vol 4) now warns about in its closing slides, where Progress 8 turns out to be something inspectors read before they visit.

What to use instead. The same data has much better candidates:

Grouping factor Groups Comment
LANAME (local authority) 152 Genuine nesting, policy-relevant, matches the Brighton framing
DfE region 25 Good higher level; picks up the London effect from Vol 2
OFSTEDRATING 5 Currently used

Local authority is the natural choice: it is a real administrative hierarchy, it has 152 groups, it connects directly to the Brighton and Hove question the series opens with, and (1 | region/LANAME) gives a genuine three-level model to demonstrate nesting properly.

I fitted both to check this is not just theory. With log(ATT8) ~ log(FSM):

Model Between-group variance Residual variance
(1 \| region/LANAME) region 0.00669, LA-within-region 0.00261 0.01354
(1 \| OFSTEDRATING) + (1 \| LANAME) Ofsted 0.01206, LA 0.00593 0.01101

Both fit fine. The nested version is the one that tells a story about geography and policy; the Ofsted version tells a story about a grading system grading itself.

Resolved: Ofsted is kept, because five groups genuinely do make the intercept plots legible, but two slides after the ICC now say plainly why it is a strange choice and show the same null model grouped by local authority for comparison (ICC 0.177 from 151 groups, against 0.647 from 5).

5. A small but misleading sentence about shrinkage

Remember: Higher variance = Higher uncertainty, so higher variance groups have less weight in the model

What determines how much a group is shrunk is the sampling variance of that group’s mean — roughly \(\sigma^2/n_j\) — so it is mostly about group size. As written it reads as though the between-group variance \(\sigma^2_{\text{group}}\) is what pulls a group in, which is the opposite: a large between-group variance means groups are shrunk less. Suggest:

Groups with few observations have unreliable means, so they get pulled furthest toward the global average.

6. The equations label a variable the models do not use

Volume 2 writes:

\[\log(\text{Attainment8}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\log(\text{PctDisadvantage}) + \beta_2\log(\text{PctPersistentAbsence}) + \epsilon\]

and the interaction equations use PctPersistentAbsence too. But the models, the code and the interpretation all use PERCTOT, which is overall absence, not persistent absence — PPERSABS10 is the persistent-absence variable and is not in these models. The slides’ prose says “overall absence” correctly while the maths above it says something else. Rename to PctOverallAbsence throughout.

The Python mixed-models problem

Short answer: statsmodels can do it, including the crossed and nested random effects you got stuck on. You do not need to change packages.

You said you could not find Python equivalents for the R code. The place this bites is the Final Task in Volume 3’s practical, which asks students to extend the model with

lmer(log(ATT8SCR) ~ ... + (1 | OFSTEDRATING) + (1 | gor_name/LANAME), data = ...)

That is two grouping factors, one of them nested. statsmodels’ MixedLM takes a single groups= argument, so the obvious translation has nowhere to put the second factor — which I assume is exactly where you stopped.

The way through is vc_formula (variance components), which is documented but not at all obvious. Two idioms cover everything in the practical.

Nested random effects — (1 | region/LA)

Put the top level in groups and the lower level in vc_formula. Note re_formula="1" is required: without it statsmodels silently drops the group-level intercept when vc_formula is present, which is a trap worth knowing about.

m = smf.mixedlm("log_ATT8 ~ log_FSM",
                data=d,
                groups=d["region_name"],
                re_formula="1",                          # region intercept
                vc_formula={"LA": "0 + C(LANAME)"}       # LA within region
               ).fit()

Crossed random effects — (1 | OFSTEDRATING) + (1 | LANAME)

There is no group hierarchy, so put everything in vc_formula and give groups a constant column:

d["one"] = 1
m = smf.mixedlm("log_ATT8 ~ log_FSM",
                data=d,
                groups=d["one"],                         # one group: all rows
                re_formula="0",                          # no group intercept
                vc_formula={"ofsted": "0 + C(OFSTEDRATING)",
                            "la":     "0 + C(LANAME)"}
               ).fit()

It gives the same answers as lme4

I ran both against lmer on 3,186 schools. These are the actual outputs:

Two notes for the practical: statsmodels orders the variance components alphabetically in the output (la before ofsted), regardless of dictionary order; and it reports variances where lmer’s default print shows variance and standard deviation.

Why not pymer4

You offered to refactor around pymer4, and I tested it properly before answering. It does work on this machine, but the cost is high:

  • pip install pymer4 alone is not enough — its dependency metadata is incomplete, and it additionally needs polars, rpy2 and great_tables installed by hand
  • rpy2 needs R_HOME set and R_HOME/bin/x64 added to the DLL search path on Windows, or R’s stats.dll fails to load
  • it has to be run with the project’s renv profile disabled, or R exhausts its cons memory on startup
  • it then failed on an R-side version conflict (rlang 1.1.6 installed, ≥ 1.1.7 required)

And the decisive objection: pymer4 is a wrapper around R’s lme4 via rpy2. Every student would need a working R installation and a consistent R package library and the Windows DLL path set — which defeats the purpose of offering a Python route at all. The students most likely to choose the Python tab are precisely those least likely to have a working R toolchain.

I installed all of it to test, confirmed the above, and then removed pymer4, rpy2, polars, great_tables and tzlocal again so the virtual environment still matches requirements.txt.

Structural observations

Python coverage collapses exactly where it is needed most

Counting code chunks by language:

Practical R chunks Python chunks Parity

A student who picks the Python tab gets a good experience in week one and is increasingly abandoned thereafter. Given that the fix above removes the main technical blocker in Vol 3, closing this gap is now mostly transcription work rather than research.

A 65 MB download on a personal Dropbox link

The Volume 3 lecture reads edubasealldata20241003.csv from a Dropbox share. I checked: it is live and returns 64.8 MB. That is a single point of failure owned by one person’s account, fetched on every render, and if a class of students hits it at once a free Dropbox account can be rate-limited. Worth moving into sessions/data/ with the rest, or reducing to just the columns the lecture uses.

Volume 3 switches outcome variable mid-lecture

The null-model section says it is partitioning the variance in Progress 8 (P8MEA), but the worked models and the practical use Attainment 8 (ATT8SCR). Pick one. Progress 8 is arguably the better choice for a multilevel treatment since it is already adjusted for intake, but whichever you choose it should be consistent.

Volume 3 carries a lot of hidden code

multilevel_regression_lecture.qmd has 5 chunks set eval: false and 8 set include: false; the practical has 3 and 7. Some of that is deliberate, but several of the include: false chunks in the practical’s Final Task are substantial models whose output never appears. Worth a pass to work out which are scaffolding and which are dead.

Overlap with the new GLM lecture

Vol 4 now covers three things these lectures also touch, and the duplication is worth resolving deliberately rather than by accident:

  • Overfitting — Vol 1 does it with the three Brighton schools, Vol 4 with an interpolating spline. Both are good and they are complementary; a one-line cross-reference in each direction would help.
  • Comparing against a baseline — Vol 4 makes this a rule. Vols 1–3 never compare a model against doing nothing, which would be a natural addition to the Vol 2 evaluation task.
  • Circularity of the predictors — Vol 4 closes on it. As noted above, Vol 3’s choice of grouping factor is an unflagged instance of the same problem, and connecting the two would strengthen both.

One thing I would not change

The series takes a position. It opens with a council that has made a policy decision, builds a model that appears to support it, and then spends two and a half lectures showing why the first answer was too simple — small samples, confounding, omitted geography, clustered errors — without ever concluding that the council was simply wrong. That argumentative spine is rare in quantitative methods teaching and it is what will make students remember the statistics. Everything above is in service of it.
