Recap

  • Over three weeks we built a regression model up from a single scatter plot to a linear mixed effects model with random intercepts and random slopes
  • We learned a recipe - understand your data, start simple, add complexity only when it earns its place, and check your assumptions at every step
  • Last week we relaxed one particular assumption: that our observations were independent. Schools sit inside local authorities, and that grouping matters

But one thing never changed

  • Every single model we have fitted has had a continuous outcome variable
  • Attainment 8. Progress 8. Numbers that can take any value, that are roughly bell-shaped, and that happily sit on either side of a straight line
  • This choice let us use Ordinary Least Squares throughout - but we know that our data don’t always look like this
  • What happens when the thing you want to explain is a yes/no, a category, or a count?

This week’s session aims

  • Understand why OLS fails when the outcome is not continuous, and see it fail
  • Meet the Generalised Linear Model (GLM) - one framework, three moving parts
  • Logistic regression for binary outcomes, then ordinal and multinomial versions for categories
  • Poisson regression for counts, offsets for rates, and what to do about overdispersion
  • Throughout: can we predict a school’s Ofsted rating from everything else we know about it?

This is not seven new methods. It is one method, with a slot you fill differently depending on what you are trying to explain.

What actually changes

Every GLM has exactly three pieces. You already know the middle one.

  1. The random component - what distribution does the outcome follow? (Normal, binomial, Poisson…)
  2. The linear predictor - \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2 + ...\) . This is identical to everything we have done for three weeks
  3. The link function - the bit of maths that connects the two, so that a straight line on one scale becomes a sensible prediction on the other

OLS is just the GLM where the distribution is Normal and the link function does nothing at all.

What a link function actually does

The right-hand side of any regression is a straight line, and a straight line will cheerfully produce any number at all. The thing we are trying to predict usually cannot.

Outcome Its natural range Link stretches it with To get back, apply
A continuous number \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\) nothing needed nothing
A probability 0 to 1 \(\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\) the logistic curve
A count 0 to \(+\infty\) \(\log\) \(\exp\)

The link is a translator. It stretches a squashed scale out onto the whole number line, so the straight line has somewhere sensible to live. The top row needs no stretching - which is exactly why OLS is a GLM with the boring link.

Careful - this is not the same as logging your data

Back in Vol 1 we fixed a curved relationship by taking logs of Attainment 8 and of % disadvantaged, then fitting OLS to the transformed values.

Transforming the data

\[\log(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_i + \epsilon_i\]

  • We change every observation
  • Then fit an ordinary model to the new numbers
  • Errors are assumed Normal on the logged scale

A GLM

\[\log\big(E[y_i]\big) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_i\]

  • The data are left completely alone
  • The link is applied to the expected value
  • The distribution does the work the transformation used to

Which matters, because you cannot transform a binary outcome. A school is rated below Good or it is not - and \(\log\left(\frac{0}{1-0}\right)\) is undefined. There is nothing to take the log of.

The GLM never asks you to. It models the probability behind the 0s and 1s, and leaves your data exactly as you found them.

Can we predict School Ofsted ratings?

Our question for today

  • We have used Ofsted ratings as a predictor and as a grouping variable already

  • Today we turn it around and make it the outcome

  • Given everything else we know about a school - its results, its intake, its absence rates, its size - can we predict what Ofsted said about it?

  • And if we can, what does that tell us about what Ofsted is actually measuring?

  • Note, Ofsted has changed how it rates schools now, so single-word ratings are no longer given

The outcome variable

3,170 schools with complete data. Note immediately how lopsided this is - we will come back to that, because it causes more trouble than anything else today.

A probability of minus fifty percent?

  • The straight line gives 457 schools a negative probability of being rated below good.
  • At a Progress 8 of +1 it predicts a probability of being rated below good of about -13% (impossible).
  • The logistic curve cannot do this - it is bounded at 0 and 1 by construction.

Where the curve comes from

We need something that takes a straight line, which runs from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\), and squashes it into \([0,1]\).

  • Start with the probability \(p\) - bounded at 0 and 1, e.g. 0.4 (40%), awkward
  • Turn it into odds, \(\frac{p}{1-p} = \frac{probability of happening}{probability of NOT happening}\) or \(\frac{0.4}{0.6} = 0.67\) - now bounded at 0 and \(+\infty\), better
  • Take the log of that (\(\log\left(\frac{0.4}{0.6}\right) = -0.40\)) - now unbounded in both directions 🎉

\[\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2 + \dots\]

  • That left-hand side is the logit, and it is the link function. The right-hand side is exactly the model we have been fitting since week 5.
  • A logit simply is a mathematical function that converts a probability (between 0 and 1) into a log-odds value that can range from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\)
  • The link-function converts the expected value of the outcome (the mean) into something a straight line can cope with

Odds are not probabilities

Worth pausing, because this trips people (including me!) up all the time.

Probability Odds In words
0.5 1.0 Evens
0.75 3.0 Three to one on
0.9 9.0 Nine to one on
0.1 0.11 Nine to one against

Probabilities are bounded above. Odds are not. That is the whole reason we work with them.

And odds are not odds ratios

Odds describe one situation

\[\text{odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}\]

“This school’s odds of being rated below Good are 0.10”

An odds ratio compares two

\[\text{OR} = \frac{\text{odds}_A}{\text{odds}_B}\]

“…which is 0.07 times the odds of a school one Progress 8 point worse”

  • Easily the most common mistake in reading logistic output.
  • The odds are the raw ingredient;
  • the ratio is what you get when you put two of them side by side - and the odds-ratio is what a regression coefficient hands you.

Why the coefficient is a ratio

Our model says the log-odds are a straight line:

\[\log(\text{odds}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\]

  • At \(x\), the log-odds are \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x\)
  • At \(x+1\), they are \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x + \beta_1\)
  • So the difference in log-odds is just \(\beta_1\)
  • And subtracting logs is the same as dividing…

\[e^{\beta_1} = \frac{\text{odds at } x+1}{\text{odds at } x}\]

A ratio of two odds - because the coefficient was a difference of two log-odds all along.

See it in our own data

Progress 8 Probability Odds Odds ÷ the row above
-1 0.593 1.4552 -
0 0.089 0.0979 0.067
1 0.007 0.0066 0.067
2 0.000 0.0004 0.067

The coefficient on Progress 8 is -2.70, and \(e^{-2.70}\) = 0.067.

That is the last column, every time. One coefficient, one odds ratio, valid wherever you start from. Now look at the probabilities - they fall 0.59 → 0.09 → 0.007, doing nothing so tidy. Which is exactly why “the probability falls by X” is never a safe reading of a logistic coefficient.

  • It means the log-odds fall by that much, for every one-point increase in Progress 8

One extra Progress 8 point multiplies the odds of being rated below Good by about 0.11 - that is, it cuts them by roughly 89%.

Reading odds ratios

Anything whose interval crosses 1 is doing nothing detectable. Note how much work Progress 8 is doing compared with everything else.

So how good is it?

p_below <- predict(logit_below, newdata = test, type = "response")
accuracy <- mean((p_below > 0.5) == test$below_good)

Test-set accuracy: 86.2%

Not bad! Nearly nine schools in ten classified correctly.

Now watch.

The single most important slide today

A model that makes the same prediction every single time, with no data at all, scores 85.3%. Our model beats it by 0.9 percentage points.

Why accuracy lied to us

Below Good Good or better
Below Good 32 108
Good or better 23 788
  • Only 140 of the 951 test schools are actually below Good - the class is rare
  • The model catches 32 of them and misses 108
  • If you were Ofsted, trying to decide which schools to visit, this model would be close to useless at this threshold

Always, always compare your accuracy against the majority-class baseline. On imbalanced data, accuracy on its own is not evidence of anything.

But the model is not useless

Accuracy asks “did you get it right?” at one arbitrary cut-off. A better question is “do you rank schools sensibly?”

AUC = 0.818 - pick a below-Good school and a Good-or-better school at random, and the model gives the below-Good one a higher score 82% of the time. That is genuinely useful. The problem was never the model - it was the 0.5 threshold, which is a choice, not a law.

Moving the threshold

Drop the threshold and you catch more struggling schools, but more of your flags are false alarms. Which error is worse is a policy question, not a statistical one - and that is the right way round.

A second outcome, same story

Model Accuracy Baseline AUC Caught at p > 0.5
Below Good vs Good+ 86.2% 85.3% 0.818 32 of 140
Outstanding vs rest 88.3% 85.6% 0.896 52 of 137
  • Higher AUC, so the ranking is even better
  • Barely above baseline on accuracy, and still catching well under half the Outstanding schools
  • Not a quirk of one model. This is what rare outcomes do, and you will meet it in your own data

An uncomfortable question

Progress 8 is by far the strongest predictor in both models. So what have we actually built?

  • Ofsted inspectors read a school’s Progress 8 score before they visit
  • So we are not independently predicting a judgement - we are partly recovering one of Ofsted’s own inputs
  • That is circularity, and it makes the model much less interesting than it first looked

And a second problem: inspection dates in this file run from 2011 to 2024, median 2022. Some of these ratings were handed out a decade before the results we are using to “predict” them.

Ask these questions of your own models. They matter more than another decimal place of AUC.

Categories with an order

We threw information away

  • To fit a binary model, we collapsed four ratings into two
  • But Ofsted ratings have an obvious order: Inadequate < Requires improvement < Good < Outstanding
  • Treating them as a number (1, 2, 3, 4) is the mistake we warned about in week 1 - it assumes the gaps are equal
  • Treating them as unordered throws away real information

Ordinal logistic regression uses the order without assuming the spacing.

The idea: cut points on a hidden scale

We imagine a single continuous scale we cannot observe. The predictors move a school along it, and the cut points decide which rating it comes out as. Three cut points for four categories - and the model estimates them from the data rather than assuming them.

Running it

So: can we predict Ofsted?

Roughly, yes. Precisely, no. And the rare categories, not at all.

  • The model knows the difference between a likely-Outstanding school and a likely-struggling one
  • It cannot tell you which side of a single grade boundary a school will land
  • And it has essentially never seen enough Inadequate schools to learn what they look like

That is an honest, useful answer - and a far better one than a single accuracy figure would have given you.

Categories without an order

When there is no natural ordering

  • Ofsted ratings are ordered, so ordinal logistic is the right tool
  • But plenty of categorical outcomes are not: which region a school is in, which type of school a pupil moves to, which of five clusters a neighbourhood falls into
  • Multinomial logistic regression handles those - it fits a separate set of coefficients for every category against a reference

Let’s fit it to Ofsted anyway, deliberately ignoring the order, to see what ignoring the order costs us.

The comparison

library(nnet)
multi <- multinom(update(predictors, factor(rating4, ordered = FALSE) ~ .),
                  data = train, trace = FALSE)
Model Parameters Test accuracy AIC
Ordinal (proportional odds) 10 73.0% 2921
Multinomial 24 73.7% 2877
  • The multinomial buys about 0.7 of a percentage point of accuracy
  • It spends more than twice as many parameters to get it
  • AIC mildly prefers it, which tells us the proportional-odds assumption is not perfectly met

Use the structure in your data when you have it. The ordinal model gets almost the same performance from a third of the coefficients, and you can actually interpret it.

Counting things

A different kind of outcome

Let’s change the question and move up a level.

How many below-Good schools does each local authority have?

  • That is a count: 0, 1, 2, 3… never negative, never fractional
  • Most LAs have a few; some have none at all; a couple have a lot
  • A straight line will happily predict minus two struggling schools, which is not a thing

What counts look like

Discrete, non-negative, right-skewed, with a pile-up at the low end - 24 LAs have no below-Good schools at all. This is a Poisson-shaped problem, exactly as week 1 described.

But first - counts or rates?

Straight from week 1. Lancashire has 22 below-Good schools and Bury has 6.

Is Lancashire worse? It has 78 secondary schools. Bury has 13.

We need to account for how many schools each LA had the opportunity to get wrong. In a count model that is called an offset - a term we force into the model with a coefficient fixed at 1:

\[\log(\text{expected count}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \dots + \log(\text{number of schools})\]

Move it to the other side and you are modelling a rate, not a count.

Running a Poisson model

pois <- glm(n_below ~ mean_fsm + mean_abs + offset(log(n_schools)),
            data = la, family = poisson)
Term Coefficient Rate ratio p
(Intercept) -4.4903 0.011 <0.001
Mean % disadvantaged -0.0017 0.998 0.751
Mean % absence 0.2834 1.328 <0.001

The link function here is \(\log\), so exponentiating gives a rate ratio. And note the result: once average absence is in the model, average disadvantage adds nothing detectable - a confounding story of exactly the kind we met in week 6.

Checking the Poisson assumption

The Poisson distribution assumes something quite strong: that the variance equals the mean. Real data often disagrees.

dispersion <- pois$deviance / pois$df.residual
dispersion
[1] 1.306901

A value near 1 means the assumption holds. Much above 1 means overdispersion - the data are more variable than Poisson allows, your standard errors are too small, and you will think results are significant when they are not.

Here we get 1.31, which is mild. So Poisson is adequate - but we should check rather than assume.

When it is not adequate

Model AIC SE on absence
Poisson 577 0.0485
Negative binomial 575 0.0528
  • The negative binomial is the Poisson’s more forgiving cousin - it adds a parameter that lets the variance exceed the mean
  • This is exactly the distribution we met in week 1, and this is what it is for
  • Here the two models are essentially tied, so we keep the simpler one

Fit the Poisson, check the dispersion, escalate only if you need to.

The fun bit: who beats their own odds?

Given their intake, some LAs have far more struggling schools than expected and some far fewer. The residuals of a count model are not a diagnostic afterthought here - they are the finding.

One more outcome type

Proportions out of a known total

Back down to school level, and to a file we have not touched yet: what happens to pupils after Year 11?

Value
Schools 3,081

Median % sustaining a destination 94.4%
Schools at exactly 100% 48 (1.6%)
  • Each school gives us “x of n pupils went on to education, employment or training”
  • That is not really a percentage - it is a count out of a known total, which is exactly what the binomial distribution describes

Modelling it properly

dest_glm <- glm(cbind(OVERALL_DEST, not_sust) ~ PTFSM6CLA1A,
                data = dest, family = binomial)

Note the cbind() - we hand the model successes and failures, not a proportion, so it knows a school of 30 carries less weight than a school of 300.

Every 10 percentage points more disadvantage is associated with a 23% fall in the odds of a pupil sustaining a destination.

Does it matter? Compare the predictions

They agree almost exactly through the middle and diverge at the top end - the schools policy cares about most. Here OLS is not obviously broken. It is quietly wrong at the edges, and nothing in its output tells you so.

And check the dispersion again

Model Dispersion Verdict
LA below-Good counts (Poisson) 1.31 Mild - Poisson is fine
Destinations (binomial) 2.72 Overdispersed - use quasibinomial
  • Two models, two different answers. The check is not a formality you tick off
  • For the destinations model, switching to family = quasibinomial widens the standard errors by about 60% - the coefficients are unchanged, but our confidence in them was overstated

What carried over, and what didn’t

Still true

  • The recipe: start simple, add complexity that earns its place
  • Confounding, mediation, interactions
  • Multicollinearity and VIF
  • Thinking hard about what belongs in the model

Different now

  • No R² - use AIC, deviance, or predictive performance
  • Coefficients need exponentiating before they mean anything
  • Residual plots look strange and need different diagnostics
  • Accuracy is a trap on imbalanced outcomes

Where you can go next

  • Generalised additive models (GAMs) - when the relationship is smooth but not a straight line
  • glmer() - the intersection of this week and last week: a GLM with random effects, for grouped non-normal data
  • Spatial and geographically weighted regression - when the relationship itself varies over space
  • Ridge and lasso - when you have too many predictors and need the model to choose

You now have the two big extensions to OLS: last week we relaxed independence, this week we relaxed normality. Almost everything else is a combination of the two.

Conclusions

  • Look at your outcome variable before you choose a model. Week 1’s data types were not an academic exercise - they determine what you are allowed to do
  • A GLM is not a new method, it is the same linear predictor with a different distribution and a link function bolted on
  • Always compare against a baseline. A model that beats “predict the majority every time” by half a percentage point has not learned much
  • Check your assumptions - dispersion for counts, and the threshold question for classification
  • And ask where your predictors came from. Our best one turned out to be something Ofsted had already read

Today’s Practical

  • Building and interpreting your own logistic and Poisson models on the schools data
  • Practice at the bit that matters most: interpreting odds ratios and rate ratios in plain English
  • And deciding, for an outcome of your own choosing, which member of the family you actually need
