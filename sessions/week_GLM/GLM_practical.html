Good Skool / Bad Skool Breaks - a logistic regression walkthrough

Author

Adam Dennett

Published

Invalid Date

Preamble

Three volumes of the Regression Sessions have all relied on the idea that the thing we are trying to predict is a continuous number. That number can wander up and down a straight line without ever embarrassing us by predicting something mad!

It’s one of the core assumptions of ordinary least squares linear regression, but today we are going to break it. Our outcome is a yes/no: is this school rated below Good by Ofsted? A straight line is the wrong tool for that job, and the first task of the practical is to watch it fail - properly, on real data, with numbers you can point at - before we reach for a totally different tool that fixes it.

Practical aims

  1. Fit a linear probability model to a 0/1 outcome and find its impossible predictions
  2. Fit, and read, a binary logistic regression - including saying an odds ratio out loud in a sentence a non-statistician would understand
  3. Judge the fit with deviance, AIC and the pseudo-R²s
  4. Judge the decisions honestly: against a baseline, with a confusion matrix, and with ROC and AUC
  5. Take at least one of the two extensions further than the lecture went

Before you start: the two tabs

Every code block below has an R tab and a Python tab. They are not loose translations of each other - they do the same arithmetic on the same rows and produce the same numbers, to four decimal places, all the way through. If your two tabs disagree, something has gone wrong, and that is worth chasing down.

NoteKeep the whole thing reproducible

We split the data into training and test sets using the row position after sorting by URN, not a random number generator. That is deliberate: R’s sample() and Python’s random number generators will never agree with one another, and you would spend the afternoon wondering why your AUC was not mine. Sorting and taking every tenth school is boring, reproducible, and gives the same 2,219 training and 951 test schools in both languages.

Part 0: The data

Task 0 - Load the schools data and build the outcome

We use the same four DfE files as the lecture. Three things are happening here that are worth noticing rather than skimming:

  • Rows with no URN are dropped. The files carry local authority and national summary rows that are not schools. They also duplicate rows on a join, because a missing key matches another missing key.
  • Ofsted’s five published grades collapse to a binary. below_good is 1 for Requires improvement, Serious Weaknesses and Special Measures, and 0 for Good and Outstanding.
  • We keep mainstream secondaries only, so that we are comparing schools that are doing roughly the same job.
  • Also note how we generate a new binary variable from OFSTEDRATING for schools that are below Good and the rest. This is created differently in R and Python.

Part 2: Your first logistic regression

Task 4 - The recipe for reading a coefficient

Those coefficients are on the log-odds scale, and nobody thinks in log-odds. The recipe is three steps, and you will use it for the rest of your life:

ImportantThe recipe
  1. Exponentiate the coefficient. exp(coef) turns a log-odds difference into an odds ratio.
  2. The odds ratio is the multiplier for a one-unit increase in that predictor.
  3. Say it out loud. Above 1 means the odds go up; below 1 means they go down.

Step 3 is the one that is actually hard, and the one the assessment will ask you for. Here is the Progress 8 one written out:

Every one-point increase in Progress 8 multiplies a school’s odds of being rated below Good by 0.067 - that is, it cuts them to about a fifteenth.

TipQuestion

First, Progress 8 is already centred so that 0 is the national average, which makes the intercept meaningful here - it is the odds for an average school. Second, check it against the data: of the 283 schools within 0.05 of a Progress 8 of zero, what fraction really are below Good? You should find about 6%, where the model puts them at 8.9%. The model is not obliged to match the local average - it is fitting one smooth curve across the whole range, and this is where you see the price of that. Is it a price worth paying?

Part 3: More than one predictor

Task 6 - The full model, and the split

Two things happen at once here, and it is worth being clear about why.

First, we add predictors. Second - and this is new - we stop fitting to all the data. From here on the model is fitted on 70% of schools and judged on the 30% it has never seen. Everything in Part 5 depends on that separation, so we do it before we fit rather than bolting it on afterwards.

NoteWhy bother — remember the wiggly red line

The lecture showed two models fitted to fifteen points: a sensible curve, and one forced through every single point. The second had an average error of 0.00 on the data it was fitted to, and was the worse of the two on fifteen points it had never seen.

That is overfitting, and it is why a model’s performance on its own training data is not evidence. You can always improve that number by adding terms. The only honest test is data the model has never seen - which is what the next two lines create.

Task 8 - Centre the predictors, and get an intercept worth quoting

Task 7 left you with a useless intercept: the odds for a school with a cohort of one pupil, no absence and no disadvantaged pupils. No such school exists.

There is a standard fix. Subtract each predictor’s mean from itself, so that zero stops meaning none and starts meaning average. Progress 8 already arrives this way - that is why it behaved sensibly in Task 3.

Code
train_c <- train |>
  mutate(log_TPUP = log(TPUP),
         across(c(P8MEA, PTFSM6CLA1A, PERCTOT, PPERSABS10, log_TPUP, PTPRIORHI),
                ~ .x - mean(.x), .names = "c_{.col}"))

logit_centred <- glm(below_good ~ c_P8MEA + c_PTFSM6CLA1A + c_PERCTOT +
                       c_PPERSABS10 + c_log_TPUP + c_PTPRIORHI,

NoteHow to read them
  • Null deviance vs residual deviance. The first is how badly an intercept-only model does; the second is how badly ours does. The drop between them is what the predictors bought you.

Extension A - Take ROC back to the multilevel model

Last week you fitted a linear mixed effects model with local authority as a grouping variable. The obvious question is whether you can put a ROC curve on it.

WarningRead this before you try - the honest answer is ‘not directly’

You cannot run a ROC curve on last week’s model as it stands. ROC and AUC need a binary outcome: the curve is built by counting how many of the 1s you caught against how many of the 0s you wrongly flagged, and if the outcome is a continuous Attainment 8 score there are no 1s and 0s to count.

This is not a technicality to work around. It is the same point as the callout at the top of Part 5: holding data back and comparing against a baseline transfer to any model, but the scoreboard does not.

So the interesting version of this extension is not “ROC the old model”. It is: keep the grouping structure, change the outcome to a binary one, and you get a model that is both of the last two weeks at once - a generalised linear mixed model.

The Poisson model assumes the variance equals the mean. That is a strong assumption and it is usually wrong. Check it before you believe any of the standard errors.

## Preamble

Three volumes of the Regression Sessions have all relied on the idea that the thing we are trying to predict is a continuous number. That number can wander up and down a straight line without ever embarrassing us by predicting something mad!

It's one of the core assumptions of ordinary least squares linear regression, but today we are going to break it. Our outcome is a **yes/no**: is this school rated below
Good by Ofsted? A straight line is the wrong tool for that job, and the
first task of the practical is to watch it fail - properly, on real data,
with numbers you can point at - before we reach for a totally different tool that fixes it.

## Practical aims

1.  Fit a linear probability model to a 0/1 outcome and find its impossible
    predictions
2.  Fit, and *read*, a binary logistic regression - including saying an odds
    ratio out loud in a sentence a non-statistician would understand
3.  Judge the fit with deviance, AIC and the pseudo-R²s
4.  Judge the **decisions** honestly: against a baseline, with a confusion
    matrix, and with ROC and AUC
5.  Take at least one of the two extensions further than the lecture went

## Task 4 - The recipe for reading a coefficient

Those coefficients are on the log-odds scale, and nobody thinks in log-odds.
The recipe is three steps, and you will use it for the rest of your life:

::: {.callout-important title="The recipe"}
1.  **Exponentiate the coefficient.** `exp(coef)` turns a log-odds
    difference into an **odds ratio**.
2.  **The odds ratio is the multiplier for a one-unit increase** in that
    predictor.
3.  **Say it out loud.** Above 1 means the odds go up; below 1 means they
    go down.
:::

# Part 3: More than one predictor

## Task 6 - The full model, and the split

Two things happen at once here, and it is worth being clear about why.

First, we add predictors. Second - and this is new - we **stop fitting to all
the data**. From here on the model is fitted on 70% of schools and judged on
the 30% it has never seen. Everything in Part 5 depends on that separation,
so we do it before we fit rather than bolting it on afterwards.

::: {.callout-note title="Why bother — remember the wiggly red line"}
The lecture showed two models fitted to fifteen points: a sensible curve, and
one forced through every single point. The second had an average error of
**0.00** on the data it was fitted to, and was the **worse** of the two on
fifteen points it had never seen.

That is overfitting, and it is why a model's performance on its own training
data is not evidence. You can always improve that number by adding terms. The
only honest test is data the model has never seen - which is what the next two
lines create.
:::

## Task 8 - Centre the predictors, and get an intercept worth quoting

Task 7 left you with a useless intercept: the odds for a school with a cohort of one pupil, no absence and no disadvantaged pupils. No such school exists.

There is a standard fix. **Subtract each predictor's mean from itself**, so that zero stops meaning *none* and starts meaning *average*. Progress 8 already arrives this way - that is why it behaved sensibly in Task 3.

::: {.multicode}
#### ![](../images/Rlogo.png){width="41" height="30"}

```{r}
#| label: centre-r

train_c <- train |>
  mutate(log_TPUP = log(TPUP),
         across(c(P8MEA, PTFSM6CLA1A, PERCTOT, PPERSABS10, log_TPUP, PTPRIORHI),
                ~ .x - mean(.x), .names = "c_{.col}"))

logit_centred <- glm(below_good ~ c_P8MEA + c_PTFSM6CLA1A + c_PERCTOT +
                       c_PPERSABS10 + c_log_TPUP + c_PTPRIORHI,
                     data = train_c, family = binomial)

# the intercept, three ways
c(log_odds = coef(logit_centred)[[1]],
  odds     = exp(coef(logit_centred)[[1]]),
  prob     = plogis(coef(logit_centred)[[1]]))
```

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}

## Task 9 - Deviance, AIC and the pseudo-R-squareds

There is no R-squared here, because there are no residuals in the sense OLS
means. What we have instead is a set of numbers built on the **likelihood** -
how surprised the model is by the data it saw.

Every one of these is already sitting in the output you printed at Task 6.
The point of this task is to find them there, not to compute them afresh.

```

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}

print(logit_full.llf)          # log-likelihood

print(logit_full.null_deviance)
print(logit_full.deviance)

print(logit_full.aic)

# statsmodels has no built-in pseudo-R2 for GLM, so compute them directly
n   = logit_full.nobs
cs  = 1 - np.exp((logit_full.llnull - logit_full.llf) * 2 / n)
nag = cs / (1 - np.exp(2 * logit_full.llnull / n))
print({"Cox & Snell": round(cs, 4), "Nagelkerke": round(nag, 4)})
```
:::

::: {.callout-warning title="Python trap — `.prsquared` is not the number you just computed"}
`smf.glm()` has no `.prsquared` attribute. `smf.logit()` **does**, and it is
very tempting to reach for it instead of writing those two lines out.

It is **McFadden's** pseudo-R², which is a different measure built a different
way. Fit this model both ways and compare:

```python
lg = smf.logit("below_good ~ P8MEA + PTFSM6CLA1A + PERCTOT + PPERSABS10 "
               "+ np.log(TPUP) + PTPRIORHI", data=train).fit(disp=0)
print(lg.prsquared)
```

You should get **0.2416**, against the **0.3264** Nagelkerke you computed
above. Same data, same fit, two numbers with the same nickname. Whichever you
report, **name it**.

R users are not immune: `performance::r2_mcfadden()` sits right next to
`r2_nagelkerke()` and will give you the same 0.2416.
:::

::: {.callout-note title="How to read them"}
-   **Null deviance vs residual deviance.** The first is how badly an
    intercept-only model does; the second is how badly ours does. The drop
    between them is what the predictors bought you.
-   **AIC** is the log-likelihood with a fine for each extra parameter. It is
    only meaningful **compared with another model on the same data** - an AIC
    on its own means nothing at all.
-   **Cox & Snell** cannot reach 1, even for a perfect model. **Nagelkerke**
    rescales it so that it can. Neither one is "the proportion of variance
    explained", whatever your software calls it.
:::

## Extension A - Take ROC back to the multilevel model

Last week you fitted a linear mixed effects model with local authority as a
grouping variable. The obvious question is whether you can put a ROC curve on
it.

::: {.callout-warning title="Read this before you try - the honest answer is 'not directly'"}
**You cannot run a ROC curve on last week's model as it stands.** ROC and AUC
need a **binary** outcome: the curve is built by counting how many of the 1s
you caught against how many of the 0s you wrongly flagged, and if the outcome
is a continuous Attainment 8 score there are no 1s and 0s to count.

This is not a technicality to work around. It is the same point as the
callout at the top of Part 5: holding data back and comparing against a
baseline transfer to any model, but **the scoreboard does not**.

So the interesting version of this extension is not "ROC the old model". It
is: **keep the grouping structure, change the outcome to a binary one, and
you get a model that is both of the last two weeks at once** - a generalised
linear *mixed* model.
:::

exactly the way `glm()` was `lm()` with a `family` argument.

::: {.callout-note title="A real difference between the two languages"}
This is the one place in the practical where the tabs do **not** give you the
same numbers, and you should know why.

`glmer()` fits by maximum likelihood. statsmodels' `BinomialBayesMixedGLM`
fits by **variational Bayes** - a different estimator of a differently
specified model - so the coefficients land close but not identical, and the
summary reports posterior means rather than estimates and standard errors.
The local authority standard deviation comes out at about **0.43** from
`glmer()` and **0.45** from statsmodels - close enough that the conclusion is
the same either way, which is the point.

Python's mixed-model support is genuinely thinner than R's - and you will
feel it in A2, where there is no `predict()` that takes new data *and* the
random effects, so we rebuild the linear predictor by hand. If you need
mixed models seriously in Python, `pymer4` (which calls lme4 underneath) and
`bambi` are the usual answers.
:::

### A2 - Did the grouping actually help the ranking?

Here is where today's scoreboard earns its keep. Fit the same model **without**

flat <- glm(below_good ~ P8MEA + PTFSM6CLA1A + PERCTOT,
            data = train, family = binomial)

auc_of <- function(scores, actual) {
  r  <- rank(scores)
  n1 <- sum(actual == 1); n0 <- sum(actual == 0)
  (sum(r[actual == 1]) - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * n0)
}

p_glmm <- predict(glmm, newdata = test, type = "response",
                  allow.new.levels = TRUE)
p_flat <- predict(flat, newdata = test, type = "response")

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}
#| label: glmm-roc-py

import patsy
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

# statsmodels has no predict() that takes new data *and* the random effects,
# so we rebuild the linear predictor by hand: fixed part + this school's
# local-authority effect.
X_test = patsy.dmatrix("P8MEA + PTFSM6CLA1A + PERCTOT", test, return_type="dataframe")
eta    = X_test.values @ glmm.fe_mean

re_mean = glmm.random_effects()["Mean"]
re_map  = {k.split("[")[1].rstrip("]"): v for k, v in re_mean.items()}
# .fillna(0) is this language's allow.new.levels: an unseen authority
# simply gets the average intercept
eta += test["LANAME"].map(re_map).fillna(0.0).values

p_glmm = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))

flat   = smf.glm("below_good ~ P8MEA + PTFSM6CLA1A + PERCTOT",
                 data=train, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit()
p_flat = flat.predict(test)

print({"mixed": round(roc_auc_score(test["below_good"], p_glmm), 4),
       "flat":  round(roc_auc_score(test["below_good"], p_flat),  4)})
```

:::

::: {.callout-tip title="Question - and do not assume the answer"}
The mixed model gets **`r sprintf("%.4f", auc_mixed)`**; the flat one gets
**`r sprintf("%.4f", auc_flat)`**. Modelling `r n_la` local authorities bought
you **`r sprintf("%.3f", auc_mixed - auc_flat)` of AUC**.

1.  Was the random intercept worth it? Answer for an *explanatory* aim and
    for a *predictive* aim separately - the honest answers differ.
2.  `allow.new.levels = TRUE` - and `.fillna(0)` in the Python - is doing
    something specific when a test-set school sits in a local authority the
    training set never saw. What? And why does that limit how much a
    group-level model can ever buy you on held-out data?
3.  The LA standard deviation is `r sprintf("%.2f", la_sd)` on the log-odds
    scale, which is not nothing. How can there be real variation between
    authorities and almost no gain in ranking?
:::

### B2 - Poisson regression, with an offset

An authority with 60 schools will have more schools below Good than one with
8, for reasons that have nothing to do with poverty or absence. The **offset**
is how you tell the model to work in rates rather than raw counts: it is a
predictor whose coefficient is fixed at 1 rather than estimated.

::: {.multicode}
#### ![](../images/Rlogo.png){width="41" height="30"}

```{r}
#| label: poisson-r

pois <- glm(n_below ~ mean_fsm + mean_abs + offset(log(n_schools)),
            data = la, family = poisson)

summary(pois)
```

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}
#| label: poisson-py

pois = smf.glm("n_below ~ mean_fsm + mean_abs",
               data=la, family=sm.families.Poisson(),
               offset=np.log(la["n_schools"])).fit()

print(pois.summary())
```
:::

### B3 - Is it overdispersed?

The Poisson model assumes the variance equals the mean. That is a strong
assumption and it is usually wrong. Check it before you believe any of the
standard errors.

::: {.multicode}
#### ![](../images/Rlogo.png){width="41" height="30"}

```{r}
#| label: dispersion-r

sum(residuals(pois, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(pois)
```

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}
#| label: dispersion-py

print(pois.pearson_chi2 / pois.df_resid)
```
:::

You should get **1.247**. A value of 1 is what Poisson assumes; much above 2
and the Poisson standard errors are badly optimistic. 1.25 is mild.

### B4 - Fit the negative binomial anyway, and decide whether it was needed

The negative binomial adds one parameter that lets the variance exceed the
mean. Fit it, and then make it earn its place.

::: {.multicode}
#### ![](../images/Rlogo.png){width="41" height="30"}

```{r}
#| label: nb-r

nb <- MASS::glm.nb(n_below ~ mean_fsm + mean_abs + offset(log(n_schools)),
                   data = la)

c(poisson_AIC = round(AIC(pois), 1),
  nb_AIC      = round(AIC(nb), 1),
  theta       = round(nb$theta, 2))
```

#### ![](../images/python-logo-only.png){width="30"}

```{python}
#| label: nb-py

nb = smf.negativebinomial("n_below ~ mean_fsm + mean_abs",
                          data=la, offset=np.log(la["n_schools"])).fit(disp=0)

print({"poisson_AIC": round(pois.aic, 1),
       "nb_AIC": round(nb.aic, 1),
       "alpha": round(nb.params["alpha"], 4)})
```
:::

::: {.callout-important title="The result you were probably not expecting"}
Poisson AIC **573.5**, negative binomial AIC **572.4**. One AIC point, for a
whole extra parameter.

R reports `theta = 19.72` and Python reports `alpha = 0.0507`. They are the
same number: alpha is 1/theta. A **large** theta - equivalently an alpha near
zero - means the negative binomial has collapsed back towards the Poisson,
because there was barely any extra dispersion for it to model.

**The negative binomial was not needed here.** That is a perfectly good
finding, and far more useful than reaching for the fancier model by reflex.
Report the dispersion statistic, report that you checked, and keep the simpler
model.
:::

# Where this goes in your assessment

Three habits from today are worth carrying into the coursework, whatever
model you end up fitting:

1.  **Look at the outcome before choosing the model.** Binary, count,
    proportion, ordered category and continuous each have a family that fits
    them. Picking the family is a modelling decision you should justify in a
    sentence, not a default you fell into.
2.  **Never report accuracy without the baseline beside it.** Today's model
    scored 86.9%, which sounds respectable until you notice that doing
    nothing also scores 86.9%.
3.  **Say what you are for.** If you are *explaining*, judge the model on the
    data you fitted it to and interpret the coefficients. If you are
    *predicting*, hold data back and judge it there. Both are legitimate.
    Deciding which one you are doing **before** you start changes what counts
    as a good model, and a write-up that quietly switches between them is the
    commonest way to overclaim.
